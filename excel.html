Excel Things You Need to Know

Excel is one of the tools I reach for most often when working with data. I use it to organize information, explore patterns, connect tables and communicate findings. These are the practical features I find most useful—not a complete Excel course, just the skills I keep coming back to.

Data TablesUseful FunctionsPivotTablesLookupsChartsConditional Formatting
What I use most

Start with a question, then let Excel help answer it.

You don't need to memorize every function to work effectively with data. A few well-chosen tools can take you from raw rows to something useful.

01

Excel Tables: Start with Organized Data

Clean structure makes every other step easier.

Before analyzing a new dataset, I usually convert it to an Excel Table. Tables make it easier to sort and filter records, add new rows, and keep formulas consistent.

  1. Keep one header row and one record per row.
  2. Click inside your data and press Ctrl + T (on Windows), or use Insert → Table.
  3. Confirm that the table has headers.
  4. Under Table Design, give it a useful name, like SalesData.
My tip: Avoid merged cells, blank rows, and subtotal rows in the middle of your dataset. One variable per column is a simple habit that saves a lot of time later.
02

Useful Functions: Let Excel Do the Work

My small toolkit for everyday questions.

I don't try to memorize every Excel function. I focus on a few that answer questions I ask repeatedly.

FunctionWhen I use itExample
SUMIFSAdd numbers meeting criteriaTotal sales for a region
COUNTIFSCount matching recordsNumber of orders in a region
AVERAGEIFSAverage selected valuesAverage order value by region
IFClassify or flag recordsAbove or below a target
IFERRORHandle formula errorsShow a message for a failed lookup

Try these: If column A is Region and column C is Sales:

=SUMIFS(C:C,A:A,"West")=COUNTIFS(A:A,"West")

To flag a sales amount in C2 above $1,000:

=IF(C2>1000,"High","Normal")
My tip: Start with the business question, not the formula. Once you know what you want to calculate, it's much easier to choose the right function.
03

PivotTables: Learn from Your Data

My favorite way to get the big picture quickly.

When I receive a dataset, I often use a PivotTable before writing any complicated formulas. It helps me see which categories matter and where patterns might be hiding.

Questions I might explore: Which products generate the most sales? How do results compare across regions? What changes month to month?

  1. Click inside your Excel Table.
  2. Select Insert → PivotTable.
  3. Choose where to place it.
  4. Drag fields into Rows, Columns, Values, and Filters.

Try this: Put Region in Rows and Sales in Values. You now have a quick comparison of total sales by region. If Excel shows Count instead of Sum, check that the sales column contains numeric values.

My tip: Experiment. Move fields around and ask different questions. PivotTables are as useful for discovering questions as they are for answering them.
04

VLOOKUP: Connect Two Data Tables

Bring related information together using a shared ID.

Real data often comes from separate sources. For example, one table may contain sales transactions and a ProductID, while another table maps each ProductID to a product category.

Example: A2 holds a ProductID. On a sheet named Products, column A contains ProductID and column B contains Category.

=VLOOKUP(A2,Products!$A$2:$B$100,2,FALSE)

This finds the ID in the first column of the reference range and returns its category from column 2. FALSE asks for an exact match.

If you're using a newer version of Excel, XLOOKUP is another function worth learning:

=XLOOKUP(A2,Products!$A$2:$A$100,Products!$B$2:$B$100,"Not found")
My tip: Always check for missing matches, duplicate IDs, extra spaces, and numbers stored as text. A lookup is only as reliable as the IDs you use to connect the tables.
05

Charts: Make Your Findings Easier to Understand

Choose a visual that makes the comparison clear.

A simple chart often communicates a finding faster than a long table. Two chart types I use frequently are:

ChartBest useExample
Bar / column chartCompare categoriesSales by region or claims by category
Pie chartShow a few parts of a wholeShare of total volume by product group
  1. Summarize the numbers, often with a PivotTable.
  2. Select your category labels and values.
  3. Choose a chart from Insert → Charts.
  4. Add a clear title and labels; remove unnecessary clutter.
My tip: I usually prefer bar charts when accurate comparisons matter. Pie charts work best with a small number of mutually exclusive categories that add up to 100%.
06

Conditional Formatting: Highlight What Matters

Spot the highest, lowest, and unusual values quickly.

When I'm scanning a long table, I use Conditional Formatting to make patterns visible without reading every cell. It can help highlight top and bottom values, duplicates, thresholds, or gradual differences.

  1. Select the values you want to examine.
  2. Go to Home → Conditional Formatting.
  3. Try Top/Bottom Rules, Data Bars, or Color Scales.
  4. Adjust the rule to match your question.

Try this: Select a sales column and use Top/Bottom Rules → Top 10 Items to highlight the highest sales values. You can change 10 to a smaller or larger number.

My tip: Color should point to something meaningful. Too many highlights can make a spreadsheet harder—not easier—to understand.
A simple starting point

Where I would start

  1. Tables to organize the data.
  2. Functions to calculate, count, and flag records.
  3. PivotTables to summarize and explore.
  4. VLOOKUP or XLOOKUP to connect data sources.
  5. Charts to communicate findings.
  6. Conditional Formatting to spot important values.

Pick a dataset you understand, ask a few questions, and use Excel to find the answers. The goal isn't to become an Excel expert overnight—it's to become comfortable turning raw data into useful insights.
